<Section n="02" title="Prediction" id="prediction">

For binary classification, the label $y$ is 0 or 1 and the prediction $\hat y$ is the probability that $y = 1$.

<Key label="Linear input">

$$
z^{(i)} = w^T \cdot x^{(i)} + b = \sum_{j=1}^{n} w_j \, x_j^{(i)} + b
$$

</Key>

<Key label="Prediction, through the sigmoid">

$$
a^{(i)} = \hat y^{(i)} = \sigma\big(z^{(i)}\big) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z^{(i)}}}
$$

</Key>

</Section>

<Section n="03" title="Loss function" id="loss">

The loss $\mathcal{L}$ measures the error on one training example: we want $\hat y \approx y$. The index $(i)$ is left out.

<Key label="Cross-entropy loss">

$$
\mathcal{L}(a, y) = -\,y \log a - (1 - y) \log(1 - a)
$$

</Key>

Only one term is left for a given label. If $y = 1$, the loss is $-\log a$, small when $a$ is close to 1. If $y = 0$, it is $-\log(1 - a)$, small when $a$ is close to 0. Move $z$ to see both.

<LossExplorer />

</Section>

<Section n="04" title="Cost and vectorisation" id="cost">

The cost $J$ is the mean of the loss over the whole training set of $m$ examples.

<Key label="Cost">

$$
J(w, b) = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \mathcal{L}\big(\hat y^{(i)}, y^{(i)}\big)
$$

</Key>

Put the examples side by side as columns, and one pass computes them all.

<div class="note-grid">

$$
X = \begin{bmatrix}
\mid & \mid & & \mid \\
x^{(1)} & x^{(2)} & \cdots & x^{(m)} \\
\mid & \mid & & \mid
\end{bmatrix} \in \R^{n \times m}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
Y &= \begin{bmatrix} y^{(1)} & \cdots & y^{(m)} \end{bmatrix} \\
Z &= \begin{bmatrix} z^{(1)} & \cdots & z^{(m)} \end{bmatrix} \\
A &= \begin{bmatrix} a^{(1)} & \cdots & a^{(m)} \end{bmatrix} \\
B &= \begin{bmatrix} b & \cdots & b \end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
\quad \in \R^{1 \times m}
$$

</div>

<Key label="Over all the examples">

$$
\begin{aligned}
Z &= w^T \cdot X + B \\[4pt]
A &= \sigma(Z) \\[4pt]
J &= -\frac{1}{m}\, \operatorname{sum}\big(Y \odot \log A \\
&\qquad + (1 - Y) \odot \log(1 - A)\big)
\end{aligned}
$$

<p class="note-key-note">$\odot$ is the element-wise product, and sum adds up the $m$ columns</p>

</Key>

</Section>

<Section n="05" title="Gradient descent" id="gradient-descent">

Gradient descent finds the $w$ and $b$ that minimise the cost $J(w, b)$.

<ol class="note-steps">
  <li class="note-step-init">
    <span class="note-step-n">0</span>
    <div><b>Initialise</b> $w$ and $b$, zeros are fine for a single unit.</div>
  </li>
  <li class="note-loop">
    <span class="note-loop-label">Repeat until convergence</span>
    <ol>
      <li><span class="note-step-n">1</span><div><b>Prediction</b> $A = \sigma(w^T X + B)$ for all the examples.</div></li>
      <li><span class="note-step-n">2</span><div><b>Cost</b> $J$, mainly to check that it decreases from one iteration to the next.</div></li>
      <li>
        <span class="note-step-n">3</span>
        <div>
          <b>Update</b> the parameters:

$$
\begin{aligned}
w &:= w - \alpha \, \frac{\partial J}{\partial w}(w, b) \\[4pt]
b &:= b - \alpha \, \frac{\partial J}{\partial b}(w, b)
\end{aligned}
$$

        </div>
      </li>
    </ol>
  </li>
</ol>

### The partial derivatives

The cost is a mean, so its derivative is the mean of the derivatives of the loss:

$$
\frac{\partial J}{\partial w}(w, b) = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \frac{\partial}{\partial w} \mathcal{L}\big(a^{(i)}, y^{(i)}\big)
$$

For one example, with $z = w^T x + b$, the chain rule splits each derivative into small parts:

<div class="note-grid">

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a}}_{(1)} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial a}{\partial z}}_{(2)} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial z}{\partial w}}_{(3)}
$$

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial b} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a}}_{(1)} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial a}{\partial z}}_{(2)} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial z}{\partial b}}_{(4)}
$$

</div>

<div class="note-derive">

$$
\begin{alignedat}{2}
&(1) \quad & \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a} &= -\frac{y}{a} + \frac{1 - y}{1 - a} \\[10pt]
&(2) & \frac{\partial a}{\partial z} &= \sigma(z)\big(1 - \sigma(z)\big) = a(1 - a) \\[10pt]
&(3) & \frac{\partial z}{\partial w} &= x \\[10pt]
&(4) & \frac{\partial z}{\partial b} &= 1
\end{alignedat}
$$

</div>

The first two parts simplify nicely:

$$
(1) \cdot (2) = -y(1 - a) + (1 - y)\,a = a - y
$$

<Key label="For one example">

<div class="note-grid">

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w} = x\,(a - y) \quad \in \R^{n \times 1}
$$

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial b} = a - y \quad \in \R
$$

</div>

</Key>

<Key label="Over all the examples">

<div class="note-grid">

$$
\frac{\partial J}{\partial w} = \frac{1}{m}\, X \cdot (A - Y)^T \quad \in \R^{n \times 1}
$$

$$
\frac{\partial J}{\partial b} = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \big(a^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\big)
$$

</div>

</Key>

</Section>

<Section n="06" title="Regularisation" id="regularisation">

Adding regularisation often helps to prevent overfitting. L2 regularisation adds the squared norm of the weights to the cost, so large weights cost more.

<Key label="L2-regularised cost">

$$
J(w, b) = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \mathcal{L}\big(\hat y^{(i)}, y^{(i)}\big) + \frac{\lambda}{2m} \lVert w \rVert^2
$$

<p class="note-key-note">with $\lVert w \rVert^2 = \sum_{j=1}^{n} w_j^2$, the squared Euclidean norm</p>

</Key>

Each $w_j^2$ derives to $2\,w_j$, so the extra term adds $\frac{\lambda}{m}\,w$ to the gradient. The bias $b$ is not regularised.

<Key label="Gradient with L2">

$$
\frac{\partial J}{\partial w} = \frac{1}{m}\, X \cdot (A - Y)^T + \frac{\lambda}{m}\, w \quad \in \R^{n \times 1}
$$

</Key>

</Section>
